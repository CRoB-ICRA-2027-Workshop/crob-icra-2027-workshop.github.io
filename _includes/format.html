Format

CRoB is built around participation rather than long talks. Each invited talk ends with one open question for the room, and every speaker then takes a role in the afternoon, such as seeding a working-group worksheet or joining the report-back.

Hot-takes debate

Experts argue for and against two polarizing statements. Each statement receives 35 minutes: an audience vote, position statements from two speakers, a moderated audience Q&A, and a second vote. The candidate statements are:

The Generality Illusion. “The physical constraints of different robot bodies are too distinct for a generalist policy to replace a specialist body.”
The Bitter Lesson of Physical Design. “Scaling compute and foundation models will inevitably solve physical design discovery, which will reduce the need for domain knowledge and physics-informed priors.”

Working groups

Working groups stay in the room and run from a one-page worksheet on four themes: benchmark design, evaluator trustworthiness, validation evidence, and when learned models should yield to mechanics or hardware tests. Participants choose a theme and form groups of at most ten, and a theme that draws more is split in two. Every organizer and invited speaker facilitates a group, and each group has a rotating scribe who fills the worksheet below.

FieldWhat the group writes down
ProblemOne body-selection claim that could be wrong, e.g., “gripper A outranks gripper B for this task.”
EvidenceFour entries (mechanics, control, learning, fabrication), each naming the calculation, controller run, evaluator, or hardware test behind the claim.
Failure modesThe retraining, sim-to-real, or hardware condition under which the ranking would flip.
Evaluation protocolThe minimal experiment that would decide the claim, and what it costs.
Required resourcesSimulators, datasets, platforms, or fabrication capacity the protocol needs.
Open questionsWhat the group could not settle, tagged by field.

The four evidence entries give participants trained in hardware and participants trained in learning each a defined contribution, so neither vocabulary dominates the table.

Fishbowl

The day closes with a specialist-vs-generalist fishbowl that rotates audience members into the discussion seats. Together with the debate votes, it gives every attendee a way to take part in the afternoon, not only those presenting. The fishbowl draws on the working-group report-back to settle which questions remain open.

Community report

The workshop will produce a 4–8 page community report, Choosing Robot Bodies: Specialists and Generalists in Robot Co-Design. It will summarize discussion outcomes, open problems, benchmark desiderata, reporting standards, and a trustworthiness checklist for learned design evaluators derived from the worksheets. We will appoint artifact leads at the closing session and release the report publicly within four weeks of the conference. Where speakers consent, talk recordings will be hosted on this website alongside the accepted abstracts.
