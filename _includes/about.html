About

In robotics, there is currently no clear winner for a universal robot embodiment; a robot designed for locomotion is not optimized for fine-grained manipulation, and vice versa. Diversity in robot embodiments can help build generalist policies such as Octo and OpenVLA. However, these systems often assume a fixed distribution of embodiments, which constrains what the policy can learn, because morphology governs whether control is learnable at all, beyond its effect on final task performance. Co-design addresses this limitation by optimizing the physical hardware and the control system jointly from task information.

This workshop asks: what problems are better addressed with changes to physical embodiments rather than generalist control systems?

Cross-embodiment pretraining has become a default starting point, which turns every hardware decision into a question of transfer, and generative design tools now propose candidate bodies faster than any group can physically validate them. However, protocols for validating a design ranking before fabrication remain limited. We organize the day around three questions:

  1. When is a specialist body worth building, and what evidence justifies that choice over adapting a generalist policy to an existing platform?
  2. Can co-design methods that search for a task-optimal body work with policies pretrained across many embodiments?
  3. What evidence should precede fabrication, and when does a learned ranking of candidate embodiments survive controller retraining, sim-to-real transfer, and a hardware test?

Prior co-design workshops focused on the choice of design algorithm. In contrast, CRoB focuses on the decision that comes first: whether to design a new body at all, or to use a platform and policy that already exist. Together with participants, we will write a community report with guidelines for validating body-selection claims. These guidelines should carry over to any field where candidate designs are proposed faster than they can be tested, such as materials discovery or hardware accelerators.
